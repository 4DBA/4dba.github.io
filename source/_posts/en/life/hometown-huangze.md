My hometown is Huangze. My grandparents live in Guangming Village there.

From their door it is only a few steps to the embankment of the Huangze River. Above the dike the water lies full and unhurried, a mirror holding blue sky and layers of low hills. The hills are not fierce: ridges unspool, summits stay round. They seem to run on without end—yet I never feel small or afraid, because I know the busy streets of Shengzhou wait on the other side. The mountains are not endless. They never stand aloof to overawe you; they are gentle, as if waving you on. Put one foot in front of the other and you will get there. Each little crest you cross leaves the ground firmer underfoot; instead of tiring, you gather another push to go on.

Deep in the folds of those hills run many nameless creeks—I cannot name them, but my grandparents can; they know every stream and every village. The creeks cut thin lines through the soil and slip quiet through bamboo and field ridges. Water does not speak; it simply flows. The Huangze River runs on and joins the Shan.

People used to say, “Of southeastern landscapes, Yue is finest; of Yue, Shan leads.” Tang poets who came with the current, wandering the “Eastern Zhejiang Poetry Road,” rowed into Shan for exactly this gentleness. Nothing here matches the knife-edge drama of the roads into Shu—no sheer cliffs crushing the sky, no murderous waves. Look out and you see orderly blue-green hills, broad clear shoals, and streams you can see through. On the water the hills seem to walk toward you and the current guides the boat; everywhere a natural kindness and ease. When Wei-Jin scholars took boats through snow—setting out when the mood struck, turning home when it passed—they, too, must have relied on this calm, even river. It lets a person settle: not startled by the world, not lost in it.

Only later did I understand: people take after their land. It is not only Huangze. All of Zhejiang’s landscape carries this temper. Hills that are not precipitous, yet reward a long, careful walk. Waters that are not torrential, yet run year after year toward the far distance. In that mildness, a quiet toughness.

The people of Huangze live by the water, tending field and house, setting days in order without haste. Yet follow the river outward—from Shengzhou to Shaoxing—those who leave the hills to try the world carry a drive to cut a path open. This place has long honored letters and craft, and knows the weight of uniting knowledge with action. The slogans of “pragmatism,” “learning for the world,” or “doing solid work” land in village life without a whiff of empty doctrine. They are simply the plain manners this land teaches: fix your direction, plant each step firm, and like the slow mountain stream—never frantic, never stopping.

That seriousness and steadiness hide in the alleys, in food that refuses to skimp on labor.

Huangze wakes in white steam rising from bamboo steamers. The truest breakfast is the Huangze tofu bun: sheer dough around tender tofu like fresh curd, finished with a spoon of bright chili oil. The craft lives entirely in the hands—soft tofu tolerates no force; the maker’s fingers close fine, even pleats. Buns must be wrapped and steamed on the spot. When the lid lifts, hot steam hits the face. Bite a small hole while it is still scalding; broth-soaked tofu slides in, gentle heat, clear savor, warmth filling the mouth. Nothing here is rushed. How much the dough rises, how many minutes on the fire—all in the steamer’s years of judgment.

Besides the buns, mornings—and the afternoon pause from work—bring *maijue* (wheat pancakes) at the iron griddle: another kind of solid comfort. A handful of soft dough is swept and spun across a warm flat iron; the skin lifts thin as a cicada’s wing, soft yet chewy. Whatever the season grows, the board fries now: crisp bean shoots, fine-cut pork, oil-soaked vermicelli, piled steaming and rolled by the handful according to the weather. In the lull of afternoon hunger or between tasks, a big bite—thin wrap around the honest taste of the whole season’s fields.

For real meat comfort, the pig-head spring rolls at the alley mouth are the most honest street food. Soft dough wraps slow-cooked, yielding pork head meat—rind gelatinous, fat and lean in balance, oil neither dry nor cloying, soaking just so into the chew of the wrap. Pure, thorough meat fragrance. By evening, if you want a plate with wine, the white-cut mutton on the old street is indispensable. Whole skin-on mutton, no heavy spice to hide behind, only a slow steam until tender—no gamey edge—sliced neat and dipped in a dish of local soy. Quality of the raw ingredient carries the dish, through slow chewing. Food and people share one grain: keep it real, let time and patience simmer the flavor until the neighbors’ sharp tongues approve.

The town’s daytime bustle, as the sun slopes west, gathers at last into my grandparents’ steaming, never-raucous old house.

Before she retired, Grandma was director of the town kindergarten, watching over generation after generation of local children. Afterward, through my middle-school years, the threshold stayed polished by familiar feet. Neighbors would drop little ones with her to mind. At dusk, elders I barely knew—though my grandparents knew them well—would cross the door, settle into small bamboo chairs, fan themselves and chat. A warm, settled human presence always moved through those rooms.

Grandparents had two sons; my uncle’s family added a daughter. In my generation the old house held only my younger sister and me. Most winter and summer holidays of childhood we spent beside them. For us, the deepest memory of that house is bound to smells from the stove. On cold days Grandma would simmer longan with eggs over the fire: dark dried longan slow-cooked in syrup until the broth glowed warm brown; white boiled eggs took on a honeyed tint, even the yolks steeped in sweetness—the plainest, truest tonic of the old generation. When she made pumpkin cakes, we were ecstatic. Steamed old pumpkin kneaded into glutinous rice flour, golden soft rounds pressed into an oiled iron pan until they sizzled with charred freckles. Just out of the pan, scalding and soft, my sister and I hovered at the stove, too impatient to wait, snatching them and tossing them between our hands, blowing hard—the whole room filled with pumpkin sweetness and toasted fragrance.

As I grew older and the city’s pace quickened, I, too, grew restless now and then. In those moments my mind finds the water on the Huangze embankment and those round, rolling hills. The hills are not fierce; the river is not in a hurry; the old house never skimped on cooking. Those village holidays taught no grand lessons, yet like water soaking the field ridges without sound, they laid early a calm foundation under every step that followed.
