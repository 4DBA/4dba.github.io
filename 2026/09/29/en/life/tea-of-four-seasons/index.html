Tea of Four Seasons

Posted on 2026-09-29 Edited on 2026-09-30 In Life

In the height of July, a friend and I each hunted down two jasmine teas—one a famous brand everyone was talking about, the other a younger label popular with our generation—and set them side by side. Each had its charm. Brewing filled the room with an almost aggressive floral perfume, and the summer restlessness settled a little.

Then, near the end of September, something odd happened.

At my desk one afternoon, the bright jasmine scent had thinned—nothing like the sweetness of midsummer. The office stays climate-controlled; green tea and oolong hold up fine. Jasmine shouldn’t go dull that fast. I puzzled over it, even suspected a blend of stale leaves.

Then over the National Day holiday, with family at a hotel, I drank a casually steeped cup of osmanthus oolong. Chatting before dinner, with the first autumn wind, that warm osmanthus note lingered on the tongue—more healing than summer jasmine had ever been.

Only then did I understand:

It wasn’t the tea that had faded. My body had quietly changed with the season.

We never feel the seasons because the calendar turns a page. The tongue and the skin choose first.

Rewind to midsummer: the heart too scorched for slow sipping. Tea then was pure thirst, and if it also cooled the mind, all the better. Modern flower teas young people chase go without saying; my private remedy for the heat was liuyueshuang from home.

Push further back to early spring, and the mood is another story.

When winter still nips at spring, the first Longjing opens into a tender, almost pea-shoot aroma. Longjing is the opening note of small talk after returning to work and reuniting with family: will the new year bring gentle weather, or bright spring? The Jiangnan hills are changeable; early-spring sun and rain wrestle each other, sparing the new leaves long harsh light so they stay fragrant without bitterness. Talking about the weather remains the finest icebreaker at the tea table.

My Longjing comes from neighbors who are also from my hometown. They keep a little land at Mingshan Tea Farm, growing Longjing and Anji white tea. Our families are close; prices have been steady for years. But watching the quality of each year’s leaves became a gauge of human warmth: when the market runs hot and they won’t raise prices, quality inevitably slips; when the market cools and good lots are held back, they are likelier to share the finer stuff. A small cup of spring tea holds not only the weather, but the quiet understanding between people who know each other.

Modern days are sliced thin. We sit in steady temperatures, scrolling while information streams past and leaves no thought behind. Unless one day the tongue tires of summer’s heavy perfume and starts craving autumn’s warmth, we might forget that heaven and earth are still turning.

At first, I meant to compose a tidy Tea of Four Seasons, matching the seasons of tea to the seasons of a life. Popular wisdom loves that arc: soda as a child, iced americano at work, flower tea with age, and in later years a return to pure, classic tea—as if taste must travel from noise to calm, closing with a winter cup of black tea.

But following that “old-fashioned epiphany” frame, I stalled.

I could not write a winter tea. Forcing reflections on black tea to complete a neat four-part grid felt so contrived it made me uneasy.

At twenty-five, my body has not yet learned winter’s depth—and I have no interest in pretending otherwise.

Taste is not a one-way train. The popular “seasons of life” script is already folded into a single day for someone like me: bitter coffee to charge through the morning, Longjing or jasmine to quiet the afternoon, and a soda from the convenience store on the way home—no guilt at all.

The kick of sweet water, the punch of caffeine, the slight bitter of liuyueshuang, the freshness of spring leaves—if it tastes good, my body accepts it. Tea does not mean growing old; soda does not mean being childish. That is the loveliest privilege of being young.

If life need not march in lockstep, why must an essay fill spring, summer, autumn, and winter?

In the end, neither the four seasons of tea nor the four seasons of life are stern rules. Drinking tea is not filling in blanks. It is only this body, in a mouthful of leaf and vapor, belatedly claiming its own hour.
